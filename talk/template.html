01

How we measured it

One schematic carries the whole method: continuous tracking of every individual, movement speed per bee, a cosine fitted to that speed, physical contacts detected from overlap, and an agent-based model built on the same rule. If the audience follows this one slide, every later figure reads itself.

Opening slidefigures/imgs/overview_figure.png

From video of a whole colony to a single interaction rule

Two observation hives were filmed without interruption, 57 days for colony A and 100 for colony B. Every bee carried a unique tag, so identity and age are known for all 1917 and 3404 individuals. The bees foraged freely through a tube to the outside and were otherwise left alone.

  • aTracking. Detected trajectories on the comb; two bees are marked with the 14 × 6 mm rectangles used to define contact. Overlapping rectangles count as an interaction.
  • bCosinor fit. A 24 h cosine is fitted per bee per day to movement speed. Two numbers come out and get used everywhere after this: the peak time T(φ) and the fit quality R², which stands in for how pronounced the rhythm is.x: time  ·  y: movement speed [mm/s]
  • cSpeed change at contact. For every interaction, mean speed over the 30 s after minus the 30 s before, for both partners. Here the slow bee (blue) gains and the fast bee (green) loses.x: time relative to contact [s]  ·  y: movement speed [mm/s]
  • dAgent-based model. Random-walking agents in a bounded arena that hand speed to each other on contact — the minimal version of what panel c measures.
Say this We tracked every bee in the colony continuously for months, fitted a daily rhythm to each individual, measured what happens to both partners each time two bees touch, and then asked whether that one contact rule is enough to explain the colony.
02

Every age class is rhythmic, and the nest is a phase gradient

This is where the received picture gets amended. Nurses are usually described as arrhythmic; here they are rhythmic, just weakly — and rhythm properties turn out to map onto where a bee is rather than onto a task label.

Main figurespeed_transfer_paper/figure_1_colony_B.png

Rhythm strength, synchrony and peak time all track distance from the entrance

Bees were split into 5-day age cohorts. Significant daily rhythms show up in every cohort: 29 % (colony A) and 64 % (colony B) of 1–4 day-old bees, rising above 89 % and 93 % past 35 days. Mapped onto the comb, age falls off continuously with distance from the entrance, and peak activity time slides with it — from 12:20 near the entrance to 14:12 deep inside.

  • aSpeed by age group, three days. All groups are faster by day than by night; the day–night gap widens with age. Purple is young, yellow is old. Hourly bins, Gaussian-smoothed (σ = 4); bands are 95 % CIs of the unsmoothed speeds.x: date · y: movement speed [mm/s] · grey columns = night
  • bTwo individuals, three days each. A 1-day-old nurse (purple) and a 30-day-old forager (green). Both fits are significant, but the nurse's is shallower and noisier — the visual case for "weakly rhythmic, not arrhythmic". Missing points on the nurse's day 0 are just the introduction time.x: date · y: movement speed [mm/s] · dashed = cosinor fit
  • cShare of rhythmic bees by age. Monotonic increase with age — and no cohort near zero.x: proportion [%] · y: age cohort [days]
  • dMedian age over the comb. Old bees hug the entrance (red arrow, lower left); the youngest sit in the brood area at the centre.comb view in mm; contours = median age [d]
  • eRhythm parameters vs. distance to entrance. The load-bearing panel. Four min–max normalised traces — peak phase, synchronicity (SD of phase), median R², median age — with their raw scales on the four right-hand axes. Colour saturation encodes how many bees contributed. Further from the entrance means later peak, weaker rhythm, poorer synchrony.x: distance to entrance [mm] · y: normalised parameter value
Say this There is no clean nurse-versus-forager split. There is a gradient: the deeper into the nest a bee sits, the weaker her rhythm, the less synchronised she is with her nestmates, and the later in the day she peaks — by up to two hours.
Pre-empt this

The distance relationship in e is not strictly monotonic. The youngest bees sit around 300 mm out (the brood area) and age rises again beyond that. Say it before someone points at the tail: what keeps going in one direction past the brood area is the peak time, which is the claim being made.

03

Bumping transfers speed, and it is directional

A gradient that builds over hours rules out the fast Zeitgebers — light leaking in, odour, temperature, vibration all spread near-instantly. Something slow and behavioural is left. This act tests the obvious candidate: bees running into each other. An interaction is defined by overlap of the two 14 × 6 mm body rectangles; the effect is mean speed over 30 s after minus 30 s before, computed with each partner taken as focal in turn.

Main figurespeed_transfer_paper/figure_2_colony_B.png

Fast bees give speed to slow bees, and where the contact lands decides who gains

The null model resamples two bees at random on the comb at each interaction time and measures their speed changes the same way. Against it, the real data differ in 35 of 36 cells for colony B and 34 of 36 for colony A (Welch's t-test, p < 0.05).

  • aSpeed × speed, real vs. null. Both bees binned into six speed quantiles at contact onset; colour is the focal bee's speed change. The fastest bees (quantile 6) lose the most, especially against slow partners; slow bees gain.x: focal bee's start-speed quantile · y: partner's start-speed quantile · colour: focal speed change [mm/s]
  • bPhase × phase, real vs. null. Same layout, binned by cosinor peak time instead. Late-peaking bees gain the most, and gain most from early-peaking partners — which are the older, more rhythmic, entrance-side bees. The null panel is flat. Note the effect is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than in a: speed is the driver, phase rides along with it.x / y: peak-time quantile · colour: focal speed change [mm/s]
  • cWhere the focal bee was touched. Speed change projected into the focal bee's own body frame. Contact at the head is negative (dark), contact at the rear is positive (yellow). Circle area is the number of observations.x / y: position around the body [mm], bee drawn to scale · colour: speed change [mm/s]
  • dWhat happened to the partner. Same coordinate frame, partner's speed change. Mirror image of c: being hit by a head accelerates you, running into someone's rear slows you. Bees mostly move forwards, so this is exactly a nudge from behind.x / y: position around the focal bee [mm] · colour: partner's speed change [mm/s]
Say this When a bee bumps into the back of a nestmate she loses speed and the nestmate gains it. It is not generic arousal from being touched — the sign of the effect depends on which end of the body the contact lands on.
Two things to have ready

The null model shows the same qualitative pattern. Fast bees slow and slow bees speed up even in shuffled data, because speed is bounded — a bee at top speed can only decelerate, a resting bee can only accelerate. The claim rests on the difference from that baseline, not on the raw direction. Say it yourself before it is asked.

Avoid the momentum analogy. The preprint calls this "analogous to particles transferring momentum"; a reviewer objected and the manuscript is being revised, because nothing is conserved here — total speed is not preserved across a contact. Nudging or handing over activity is safer language.

Check the Fig. 2b statistic. main.tex still carries an unresolved note at that sentence — "36 out of 36 with p < 0.01 ??" — so the count for the phase panel is not settled. Don't quote a number for b until it is.

04

Activity travels through the nest as a wave

Local rule established, this act shows what it produces at colony scale — and adds the piece that only whole-colony tracking makes possible: following activating contacts backwards in time to find who started them.

Main figurespeed_transfer_paper/figure_3_colony_B.png

The entrance leads, the nest core follows, and the chains of contacts point back to the entrance

For c: for a given young rhythmic bee, the interactions that accelerated her were identified, then the interactions that accelerated those partners, and so on backwards in time. That yields a tree with the target bee at the root and driver bees at the leaves. Those drivers peak earlier, have higher R², and are older than the rest of the nest (Brunner–Munzel, p < 0.001).

  • aObserved peak time over the comb. Median phase per location, entrance at the lower left. Yellow (early) at the entrance grading to dark (late) in the core — roughly 12.3 h to 14.7 h.comb view [mm] · colour: hour of peak activity
  • bSame map from the model. Crosses mark where each group starts: group 1 rhythmic at the entrance (lower left), group 2 non-rhythmic in the core. The gradient is emergent — group 2 has no rhythm of its own and no access to the driver except through contact.simulated arena [mm] · colour: hour of peak activity
  • cWhere activating chains begin. Average depth of interaction-tree vertices per location. High values (yellow, at the entrance) mean many further interactions sit downstream of a bee in that spot.comb view · colour: average vertex depth
  • dWhen and where transfer is strongest. Post-interaction speed change against time of day and distance to the entrance. The bright block is 9 am–3 pm within ~15 cm of the entrance; further in, the effect is both weaker and later. This panel is the wave.x: hour of day · y: distance to hive exit · colour: post-interaction speed change [mm/s]
Say this Trace every activating contact backwards and the chains converge on the entrance. Activity is not switched on everywhere at once — it starts where the foragers are and works inwards, arriving in the brood nest about two hours late.
Note on panel d axes

The y-axis on d is labelled in millimetres but the range runs 0–14, i.e. it is in centimetres — matching the "< 15 cm" claim in the text. Same for the axes of c. Worth fixing in the figure before it goes on a screen at conference size.

05

Is the model just tuned to give the answer?

Backup slides — this material was produced for the revision and answers the question that always comes from the floor. The model has four structural ingredients besides the external driver: walls, a location preference (homing), speed transfer, and turning noise. Every one was removed, alone and in all 24 combinations, five seeds each, with a 200-permutation test for whether group 2 ends up rhythmic.

Model outputsimulation/visualizations/figure_3b_simulated.png

The simulated phase map, regenerated from the current model code

Same panel as Fig. 3b, re-run after the model was cleaned up for the revision — an undocumented extra homing term that applied only to group 1 was found and removed. The gradient survives it. Use this version rather than the preprint panel if anyone asks whether the published figure still reproduces.

Say this Two groups, one rule between them. Nobody tells group 2 what time it is except the agents bumping into them.
Backup slidesimulation/visualizations/ablation_single_factor.png

Remove speed transfer and the effect vanishes; remove anything else and it survives

The bar is the fitted amplitude of the non-rhythmic group's mean speed (mean ± SEM, n = 5 seeds); grey means the permutation test found no significant rhythm. Baseline sits at 0.220. Cutting speed transfer drops it to 0.0017 and it goes non-significant. Homing (0.227) and turning noise (0.225) change nothing at all. Walls matter but only as an amplifier — 0.071, still significant — because confinement raises the contact rate. Homogenising the starting positions actually helps (0.274): mixing the groups produces more early cross-group contacts.

Say this We took the model apart piece by piece. Speed transfer is the only piece the result depends on — everything else changes how strong it is, not whether it happens.

Have the unit conversion ready

Δφ is in degrees of the simulated cycle, not hours. The baseline 14.8° is about 1.0 h on a 24 h cycle, against roughly 2 h in the bees. So the model reproduces the direction and existence of the lag, not its magnitude — and the sensitivity sweeps in act 6 show the lag is tunable anyway. Better to state that yourself than to have the arithmetic done from the floor.

06

How robust is it to the parameters?

Three tunable parameters: agent density d, day length Tday, and the decay γ of the speed-transfer memory. Each was swept alone with the others at baseline (d = 1.0, Tday = 100, γ = 0.8), five seeds per point. The transfer never disappears anywhere in these ranges — what moves is how strong it is and how long it takes.

Backup slidesimulation/visualizations/sensitivity_density.png

More crowding means a stronger and faster-arriving rhythm

Density sets how many partners are available, so it directly controls the contact rate. Amplitude climbs from 0.07 at d = 0.25 to 0.37 at d = 3.0, and the lag shrinks from about 17° to 9°. Both are monotonic and both are the direction you would predict, which is the point of showing it.

Say this If the mechanism is contact, then packing the arena tighter should make the rhythm arrive stronger and sooner. It does.
Backup slidesimulation/visualizations/sensitivity_decay.png

Memory of past contacts sets both the size of the effect and the length of the delay

γ is a leaky memory: the accumulated transfer is multiplied by γ each step, so γ = 0 means only the instantaneous contact counts. Amplitude rises from 0.06 at γ = 0 to 0.30 at γ = 0.9, and the lag roughly triples, 7° to 21° — long memory acts as inertia, so it takes longer for the current driving phase to win out. The result that matters: transfer stays significant (p < 0.01) even at γ = 0. The mechanism does not need integration over time.

Say this Even with no memory at all — where only the contact happening right now counts — the rhythm still transfers.
Backup slidesimulation/visualizations/sensitivity_day_duration.png

Day length leaves the effect size alone but rescales the reported lag

Amplitude is essentially flat above Tday = 75 (A2 ≈ 0.22): the steady-state transfer does not depend on temporal resolution. The lag, though, falls from about 31° at Tday = 50 to 2° at 800 — because the propagation delay is roughly constant in simulation steps, so a longer cycle turns the same delay into a smaller fraction of 360°.

This explains an apparent contradiction

The 2-D sweeps below report ~2.7° at the nominal baseline cell while the 1-D sweeps report ~14.8°. They are not inconsistent: the 2-D sweeps were run at Tday = 400 (run_phase_shift_sweep.py:46), the 1-D sweeps at 100, and this panel is exactly the rescaling that accounts for the difference. Worth knowing before both sets of numbers end up on the same slide.
